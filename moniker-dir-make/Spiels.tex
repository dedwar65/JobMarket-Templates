\section{\large Job Market Paper Spiel}


\noindent
My job market paper is at the intersection of wealth inequality and macroeconomics. The core question I answer is: \textit{Does allowing for heterogeneous returns in a standard consumption-saving framework help explain the skewed distribution of wealth observed in the data?} 

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent
The early literature on wealth inequality focused on whether skewed labor earnings alone could explain the shape of the wealth distribution. But while income is unequal, wealth is far more skewed, especially in the upper tail. Early models then tried adding stochastic returns to wealth as an exogenous process, which did help match the distribution empirically. However, those models don't relate wealth accumulation to  household behavior.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent
My paper is in line with heterogeneous agent macro models which contribute to this discussion. I show that introducing modest heterogeneity in returns on top of labor income risk significantly improves the model's ability to match targeted and untargeted wealth moments. Unlike time preferences or risk aversion, household returns are observable. The estimated distribution of returns from my paper corresponds to empirical evidence on returns from Norwegian administrative data. 

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent
There are two key policy implications of my paper. First, capital income taxation reduce wealth inequality more than wealth taxation when returns differ across households. This is because capital income taxes shift the tax burden toward those most productive with capital. Second, from a welfare perspective, in the partial equilibrium setting capital income taxation is preferred by the bottom six out of seven return types. This matters because wealth and capital income taxation are frequently debated in policy circles. My findings suggest that returns heterogeneity should play a central role in evaluating these proposals, both for their distributional impact and welfare consequences.

\section{\large Other papers}

As for the other chapters of my dissertation, I am currently working with HRS data to consider the relationship between trust and returns to net worth. Although there is much work on trust and the economic performance of countries, there is a recent paper which looks at trust and economic performance at the individual level. They find that there's a right amount of trust regarding maximizing an individual's income -- basically that you don't want to be too pessimistic or too trusting. My work contributes to this literature by seeking to establish a similar relationship for returns. We know that there is a persistent component to returns measured in many datasets, and I'd like to see what happened to the distribution of this persistent component when trust is accounted for. It may be the case that the two are correlated. 

I also am working with a coauthor on a paper which seeks to describe the effects of energy price shocks on consumption and income inequality. There's a notable paper which identifies monetary policy shocks and shows the impact of those shocks on similar measures of consumption and income inequality. Using some data on identified supply and demand shocks to oil prices, we are in the process of doing a similar exercise. Energy prices have been more volatile relative to the prices of other goods, especially in recent years. This, along with the fact that energy consumption represents a larger share of income for poorer households, make studying the effects of energy price shocks of interest to inequality research.
